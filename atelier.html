A Curatorial Essay · Part II of III

The Atelier
& The Loom

On the technique that makes yúnjǐn unique — and the institution that, today, holds it.

The huā lóu, or "flower-tower," is among the most complex hand-looms in the textile history of any culture. It stands four metres tall, five-and-a-half metres long, comprises 1,924 individual components, and requires two skilled craftspeople to operate together. Working eight hours a day, the pair produces five to six centimetres of brocade. There is no way to make it go faster. This is not inefficiency; it is the technique. The fabric records, in its density of figured weft, the simultaneity of two people thinking together.

The four operations

The making of a yúnjǐn brocade divides into four phases. The first is the preparation of the silk: degumming, dyeing, and the separation of warp and weft into their respective threads. The dyes were historically mineral and vegetable; the atelier still works with both, though chemically stable dyes are used where lightfastness demands.

The second is the design phase — the drafting of the yìjiàng, or grid-cartoon, on which the figured pattern is plotted square by square. A yúnjǐn cartoon for a single garment can occupy a full year of preparation.

The third is the knotting of the huā běn, the pattern-script. Each colour in the design is assigned a group of warp threads; each group is encoded into a system of vertical "foot" cords and horizontal "ear" cords, knotted in sequence. The pattern-script hangs above the loom and is read, line by line, by the upper weaver during weaving. It is, in effect, a programmed memory of the design — the textile equivalent of a Jacquard card, two centuries before Jacquard.

The fourth is the weaving proper. Here a particular technique, wā huā pán zhī — literally "scooping flowers, coiling weaves" — distinguishes yúnjǐn from all other Chinese figured silks. Where most brocades introduce a coloured weft across the entire breadth of the cloth, the yúnjǐn weaver introduces colour only at the points where it will appear in the finished pattern. The shuttle is replaced by hand-coiled small bobbins of every needed hue. The result is a fabric of immense colour richness on the front, but with the colours carried only where they are wanted — no long floating threads on the reverse.

Our partnership

SEIDENHERKUNFT works in direct partnership with the Nanjing Yúnjǐn Museum & Research Institute — a single team operating under two names, founded in 1957 as China's only specialised yúnjǐn institution. All brocade panels used in our commissions are woven in the Institute's atelier.

The Institute's standing in the field is unusual. It is the body that, over the second half of the twentieth century, recovered the full huā lóu technique from surviving senior weavers and from textile evidence preserved at the Palace Museum, the Nanjing Museum, and the Ming imperial tombs at Dingling. It has produced commissioned reproductions for major Chinese and international institutions, including the Palace Museum in Beijing and the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, and has represented yúnjǐn at cultural-exchange events in more than twenty countries — Italy, France, Belgium, Norway, the United States, Australia, Japan, Korea, and others.

In 2003 the Institute collaborated with the Palace Museum on the first major exhibition of yúnjǐn imperial textiles in Beijing. In 2009 it was the formal applicant for the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage inscription. The Museum, on the same site, was designated a national patriotic-education base by China's central propaganda department and is the only specialised yúnjǐn museum in the country. The Institute and Museum collectively hold approximately 970 yúnjǐn textile objects (including significant historical artefacts), more than 2,000 pattern-script archives — among them hànfǔ-gǎo drafts surviving from the imperial weaving bureau — and a specialised library of more than 58,000 volumes.

For SEIDENHERKUNFT this partnership means three things: every panel in every commission is woven by, or under the direct supervision of, masters trained in the full historic technique; the provenance of each panel can be traced to the atelier; and our editorial and curatorial work draws on the Institute's archive and scholarly staff. We are not a reseller. We are a European house whose entire production is sourced from a single institution — the same institution that recovered the technique and holds it today.

The recovery, and UNESCO

The fall of the Qing in 1912 dissolved the imperial workshops. For most of the twentieth century the full technique survived only in fragments — in the memories of a handful of senior weavers, in textiles preserved at the Palace Museum and the Nanjing Museum, in unbound pattern-scripts kept in private hands. The Nanjing Yúnjǐn Research Institute undertook a long and patient reconstruction: rebuilding looms from old photographs, identifying surviving dyers, persuading senior weavers to teach.

The work is one of the most ambitious craft-recoveries of the twentieth century, and it remains unfinished. Some Ming-dynasty techniques — particularly certain methods of incorporating gold-leaf strips into the weave — are still being worked out from textile analysis. The technique has, in this sense, both a long history and a recent rebirth.

In 2009 UNESCO inscribed Nanjing yúnjǐn weaving on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The citation describes the craft as one of the great achievements of human textile history.

§ The Four Phases

From cocoon to cloth.

i

Preparation

Degumming of the raw silk, dyeing in mineral and vegetable baths, separation of warp and weft.

ii

Cartoon

Drafting of the yìjiàng grid-cartoon. Each square represents a single warp-weft intersection.

iii

Pattern-Script

Knotting of the huā běn — the encoded memory of the design, suspended above the loom.

iv

Weaving

Two weavers in concert. Coloured wefts introduced only where the pattern requires. Five centimetres per day.

§ The Institute & Its Masters

The hands behind the cloth.

All brocade panels in our commissions are produced by master weavers of the Nanjing Yúnjǐn Research Institute. Senior masters are credited individually on the provenance card accompanying each piece; introductions to specific masters can be arranged for institutional partners and editorial enquiries.

A.

The senior masters

The Institute today employs senior craftspeople holding the highest national professional ranks, several of whom have given more than four decades to the loom. Their work has been commissioned by the Palace Museum, the Ming tombs at Dingling, and museums abroad. The framed-panel and dragon-pattern commissions in the present collection are from this senior atelier.

B.

The next generation

A younger generation, trained inside the Institute, produces the small-format panels best suited to the wearable objects in our collection. Many came to the loom through formal apprenticeship; some through the Institute's relationships with art and textile programmes at Nanjing's universities.

C.

The allied crafts

Nanjing róng huā silk velvet flowers, Sòng brocade, and Jīnlíng gold leaf are allied traditions, also inscribed on China's national heritage registers. We commission these from masters in workshops adjacent to the Institute. See the Other Crafts page for details.
